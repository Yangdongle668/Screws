Material-Specific Heat Treatment

Heat-treatment selection follows the alloy, required condition and applicable acceptance specification. One sequence cannot be applied to every screw.

Quench and temper

Suitable carbon and alloy steels can be processed to specified mechanical requirements. Property classes such as 8.8, 10.9 and 12.9 require an applicable specification and verification; treatment names alone do not establish the class.

Case hardening and boron steel

Carburizing and hardening can produce a hardened surface with a different core condition. A grade such as 10B21 does not by itself prescribe carburizing: specify core properties, case requirements and application.

Stainless and titanium distinctions

304/316 austenitic stainless steels are not precipitation-hardening grades. Solution annealing serves a different purpose from aging 17-4PH. Titanium and other alloys need grade-specific treatment; do not transfer a stainless cycle to them.

Stress relief and stability

Stress-relief or specialist cryogenic treatments are selected only where justified by material condition and dimensional needs. State the required final condition and review distortion, corrosion and finish compatibility.

Inputs and outputs for Material-Specific Heat Treatment

The process review begins with Custom Set Screws & Grub Screws, Shoulder & Step Screws, Custom Lifting Eye Bolt Designs and the actual drawing, not a generic equipment list. Identify the incoming stock or part condition and the dimensions that must remain controlled at this stage.

Use the process topics above to resolve quench and temper; case hardening and boron steel; stainless and titanium distinctions; stress relief and stability. Record the selected requirements in the order specification. A process name does not establish the resulting tolerance, property class or inspection coverage.

Coordinate the next operation

The next review may involve Coatings & Surface Treatments or CCD Vision & Dimensional Inspection. Check which surfaces are used to locate the part, whether the next operation changes dimensions and at what stage final acceptance is measured. Where a requirement cannot be checked after assembly or finishing, agree an intermediate check as well as final verification.

For samples involving tooling, the typical lead time is approximately 10–15 days, subject to drawing review and confirmation of material, finishing and inspection scope. The complete route determines the confirmed schedule; the duration is not a promise for each individual process.
