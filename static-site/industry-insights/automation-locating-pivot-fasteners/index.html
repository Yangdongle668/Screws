Factory automation: specifying locating and pivot fasteners for repeatable assemblies

A fastener in an automated fixture may do much more than hold two parts together. Its shoulder can locate a plate, its head can limit travel, or its shank can become the bearing surface for a moving element. Those functions make small dimensional relationships important, but “precision screw” is not a complete specification. A useful drawing explains which surfaces establish the assembly, what movement is intended and how the final condition will be checked. Without that information, a supplier can manufacture an attractive part that is dimensionally plausible yet wrong for the machine.

Industry context without unsupported performance claims

IFR’s World Robotics reporting documents the deployment of industrial robots and the installed base of automation. That evidence supports discussing automation as a real manufacturing context. It does not tell a fastener supplier what tolerance to promise or prove that a particular fixture will remain accurate. This article does not extrapolate a robot-market statistic into a ZUFEK sales forecast. It examines the engineering and purchasing questions that arise when drawing-defined screws become parts of positioning, pivoting and adjustment interfaces.

As equipment platforms evolve, designers often want common spare parts and faster replacement. Those goals can conflict with informal customisation. Two shoulder screws can have the same nominal thread and overall length while locating from different faces, carrying different coatings or providing different usable bearing lengths. The difficulty is not merely making a small part accurately. It is maintaining a clearly defined relationship between the fastener, the mating components and the approved assembly procedure through design changes and repeat orders.

Separate locating, bearing and clamping functions

A locating surface establishes position. A bearing surface permits or supports movement. A clamping surface transfers a tightening load into the joint. One screw may contribute to several functions, but the drawing should identify them separately. If the threaded portion is intended only to retain the part, do not use its nominal axis as an unexamined substitute for the required locating datum. Conversely, a close-fitting shoulder does not automatically mean that every remaining diameter needs an equally tight tolerance.

Ask what would happen if each feature moved within its allowed limits. Would the mating plate shift, would a pivot bind, or would a cover simply remain clear? That review helps direct manufacturing effort toward the features that control function. It also helps the purchaser distinguish a functional requirement from a dimension copied from an older drawing. A short assembly explanation can be more valuable than adding another decimal place to every dimension. It gives the supplier a basis for asking useful questions rather than guessing which measurements the customer actually cares about.

Review both ends of the axial stack

For a pivoted cover, the shoulder must interact correctly with the complete thickness stack. Include washers, bushings, spacers, coatings and the actual seating face. At one extreme, the stack may be clamped before the intended shoulder seat is reached. At the other, the assembly may have more axial movement than intended. A nominal shoulder length between those two outcomes is not enough; the allowed variation of the mating parts must be considered alongside the screw’s variation.

Show where the shoulder length is measured from and which final surface condition applies. If a washer changes the contact plane, treat it as part of the controlled assembly. If a threaded receiving hole has limited depth, check bottoming separately from shoulder seating. These conditions can produce similar tightening sensations while representing different failures. The assembly review should identify the desired stop and the surfaces that must remain free. That information is needed before anyone can responsibly select a shoulder-length tolerance or recommend a change in the part.

Specify the relationship between axes and surfaces

A diameter tolerance controls size; it does not by itself control every relationship that affects motion. Where the assembly depends on the shoulder’s relationship to a thread, head face or another step, identify the relevant datum system and geometric requirement. Avoid a generic note such as “all concentric” without a defined basis and acceptance method. A drawing should allow both the manufacturer and the inspector to understand what is being constrained, in which setup and relative to which functional interface.

The inspection plan must then use methods appropriate to those requirements. A two-dimensional optical profile can show accessible lengths and diameters, but it does not establish every three-dimensional relationship. A thread gauge checks a different characteristic from a rotating functional trial. A mating-part check can expose an assembly problem while remaining insufficient to identify its dimensional cause. Using these methods together can be useful, but they are not interchangeable. Agree the evidence needed for the actual drawing instead of allowing the name of an instrument to stand in for an acceptance requirement.

Decide whether grinding is necessary from the requirement

Not every shoulder screw needs grinding, and grinding is not a substitute for a coherent drawing. The decision depends on the required final size, form, surface condition, material and process sequence. Turning may produce an acceptable interface for one application; another may require an additional finishing operation. A statement that a whole product family is always ground can hide unnecessary cost or conceal which surface is actually controlled. Ask which requirement makes the finishing operation necessary and when it is performed.

If heat treatment or coating follows a precision operation, review how the final delivered condition will be achieved and accepted. A measurement on an unfinished shoulder does not automatically establish the size after later processing. Identify any machining or treatment allowance and define the surfaces protected from, or included in, a finish. This is especially important where the drawing combines a locating shoulder with a threaded interface. The quote should describe the proposed route sufficiently to show how both interfaces will reach their agreed final state.

Match prototype flexibility to production economics

An early automation programme often contains unresolved assembly details. A turned prototype route may help evaluate multi-step geometry, holes or special head features without committing immediately to a production forming tool. Once the assembly is approved and repeat volumes are clearer, cold heading or a hybrid route may deserve review. The correct comparison includes tooling, secondary operations, final finishing and inspection, not simply the machining time of the first sample.

Document which design features are frozen before authorising tooling. A later change to a locating diameter may have a different consequence from a change to a non-functional head chamfer. Ask how proposed changes will be assessed and what evidence will be required after a route change. The purchaser should not assume that a production part is interchangeable merely because it was generated from the same nominal drawing on a different route. Equally, a supplier should not treat an approved prototype as permission to change a critical interface without review.

A pivot-fixture review example

Consider a small inspection fixture with a cover that rotates on a shoulder screw and stops against a separate feature. This is an illustrative engineering exercise, not a report of a customer delivery. The buyer initially asks for a tighter shoulder diameter because the cover feels loose. Reviewing the assembly shows that some movement comes from the mating bore, some from the axial stack and some from the way the cover is supported. Tightening only the screw diameter could reduce one clearance while leaving the actual instability unresolved.

The team therefore separates radial movement, axial movement and the desired stop function. It identifies the relevant bore and seat, records their drawing limits and defines how movement will be evaluated on a sample assembly. If a revised shoulder is proposed, the supplier receives the change through a controlled drawing revision. The example does not establish a universal clearance or torque value. Its lesson is that a functional complaint should be translated into measurable interface questions before it becomes an expensive tolerance change.

Make tool access and adjustment repeatable

Adjustment screws and knurled heads introduce a different set of questions. Will the operator use a hand grip, a hex key or a powered tool? Is the adjustment made once during setup or repeatedly during service? Can the operator approach the drive squarely, and does nearby equipment obstruct a larger head? A head-size change can affect access and operating feel even when the thread remains unchanged. These are assembly requirements, not decorative choices to be settled after manufacture.

If a locking method is required, state how the adjusted position will be retained and what verification is needed. Do not assume that a different thread pitch or a rougher surface will solve every movement problem. Pre-applied locking material, a separate locking element or another arrangement changes installation and service conditions. The equipment designer should define the required behaviour and approve the selected approach. The fastener supplier can then review the resulting geometry and processing requirements without being asked to infer the entire mechanism from an isolated component photograph.

Prevent spare-part confusion

Automation equipment can remain in service across several drawing revisions. A replacement part that looks correct may belong to another revision or assembly variant. Use a controlled part identifier and document which changes affect interchangeability. Lot labels should connect supplied parts to the applicable drawing and delivery condition. Where practical, packaging should help the customer keep variants separate during stores handling and line replenishment. A consistent label can prevent a problem that cannot be detected by casual visual comparison at the machine.

When a supplier proposes an alternative material, coating or manufacturing route for continuity of supply, review it as a controlled change. The change may affect more than strength or appearance. Surface condition, friction, final fit and service-tool compatibility can all matter to the equipment. Record the approved scope and any repeat checks rather than relying on an informal statement that the part is equivalent. This protects both parties: the buyer can trace the decision, and the supplier has a clear basis for what was accepted.

Build an inspection and release plan around function

List the dimensions and relationships that control locating, pivoting, adjustment and retention. For each, identify an inspection method, the delivery condition to be measured, the sampling or checking frequency and the record required. Separate material documentation, dimensional checks, thread acceptance and any assembly evaluation. A single certificate titled “inspection report” should not be treated as evidence for characteristics that were neither measured nor specified. If an external test is required, agree its scope and responsibility before placing the order.

First-article review should confirm both the loose part and the intended interface where necessary. Repeat-lot release should preserve the characteristics that made the sample acceptable. If a customer requires every part to be checked for a visible profile, define that profile and the sorting criteria. Do not extend that claim to hidden geometry or mechanical performance without the necessary method. This distinction makes inspection meaningful and prevents the phrase “full inspection” from becoming a substitute for an actual control plan.

What a useful RFQ contains

Provide the controlled screw drawing, relevant mating details, intended movement, quantity range, material and finish basis, critical characteristics and required records. Mark unresolved questions openly. Explain whether the request is for a flexible prototype route, a production proposal or a spare part that must remain interchangeable with existing equipment. Ask the supplier to identify route assumptions and exceptions, rather than accepting a quotation that silently interprets missing information.

Repeatable automation depends on defined interfaces and controlled changes, not on describing every small component as high precision. A buyer who can explain which surface locates, which surface moves and which surface clamps has already improved the manufacturing conversation. The next task is to connect those functions to dimensions, final processing and evidence. That is how a custom fastener becomes a dependable part of an equipment design: through an explicit, reviewable specification that survives the journey from first sample to replacement order.
